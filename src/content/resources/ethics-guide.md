Research involving digital media data raises ethical questions that differ in important ways from those in traditional social science research. The data is often generated by people who did not know it would be used for research. It can reveal intimate details of personal behaviour, political beliefs, health status, and social relationships. It is produced within commercial platforms whose terms of service, data practices, and algorithmic systems create layers of complexity that do not exist in conventional research settings.

Ethics in digital media research is not simply a compliance exercise. It is a fundamental part of responsible research practice. Ethical reflection shapes study design, data collection, analysis, publication, and the long-term management of data. Researchers who treat ethics as an afterthought risk harming participants, violating community norms, and producing findings that cannot withstand scrutiny.

## The National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research

All human research conducted in Australia is governed by the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (2007, updated 2018), jointly issued by the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC), the Australian Research Council (ARC), and Universities Australia. The National Statement establishes four core principles.

**Merit and Integrity**

Research must be designed to produce knowledge that is scientifically or socially valuable. The methods must be rigorous, the researchers must be competent, and the study must be conducted honestly. In digital media research, this means that data collection methods should be appropriate for the research question, that researchers should understand the data they are working with (including its biases and limitations), and that findings should be reported accurately.

**Justice**

The benefits and burdens of research should be distributed fairly. Particular care should be taken when working with vulnerable populations or when the research may affect communities disproportionately. In digital media research, justice considerations include ensuring that research on social media data does not unfairly single out or expose particular groups, that recruitment strategies do not systematically exclude populations who lack digital access, and that the benefits of research are shared with the communities that contributed to it.

Justice also requires attention to self-selection bias. Empirical evidence shows that data donation samples are systematically skewed: donors tend to be younger (average age 48 vs. 68 for non-respondents in Strycharz et al., 2024), more female (68% vs. 52% in the population in van Driel et al., 2022), and have lower privacy concerns and lower privacy literacy than the general population (Ohme et al., 2021). These biases mean that research findings from data donation studies may not fairly represent the populations they claim to describe.

**Beneficence**

Research should aim to produce benefits that justify any risks to participants. Researchers must assess the likelihood and severity of potential harms, take steps to minimise those harms, and ensure that participants are not exposed to risks that outweigh the expected benefits. In digital media research, potential harms include privacy breaches through re-identification of individuals from supposedly anonymous data, reputational harm from publication of embarrassing, sensitive, or out-of-context content, psychological harm caused by reviewing one’s own data or being confronted with evidence of surveillance, social harm through stigmatisation of communities if research findings are misinterpreted or misused, data security incidents involving unauthorised access to personal data stored for research purposes, and third-party harm through exposure of individuals who did not consent to participation but whose data appears in a participant’s DDP (messages, tags, friend lists).

**Respect for Persons**

Research must respect the autonomy, privacy, and dignity of participants. Where participants are capable of making informed decisions, they should be given the opportunity to do so. Where they are not (e.g., children, or where consent is impracticable), additional safeguards must be in place.

In digital media research, respect for persons requires particular attention to consent. Many digital media research methods involve data produced by people who are not direct participants in the study. Distinguishing between data that requires consent and data that does not is a central ethical challenge.

## Citizen Science and the Ethics of Participation

Data donation research falls within the broad tradition of citizen science – “public participation and collaboration in scientific research with the aim to increase scientific knowledge” (ACSA, 2025). The AIO’s citizen science framework identifies three core values that govern the ethical relationship between researchers and citizen scientists. The first is participation: meaningful roles for citizens in research, not merely instrumental data provision. The second is accountability: researchers must “balance data collection with privacy considerations, accounting for the three points of data minimisation (adequate, relevant, limited to what is necessary).” The third is capacity-building: both science and society should benefit from the collaboration.

## FAIR and CARE Principles

Two complementary sets of principles guide data management in AIO research.

**FAIR Principles**

The FAIR principles ensure that research data is Findable (assigned persistent identifiers and described with rich metadata), Accessible (retrievable through standardised protocols and clear access conditions), Interoperable (using shared vocabularies and standard formats that enable integration with other datasets), and Reusable (accompanied by clear usage licenses and provenance information). FAIR principles apply to research datasets shared through the AIO Data Laboratory and to the AIO’s own data schemas (such as the LayerFlexI metadata standard).

**CARE Principles**

FAIR alone is insufficient. The CARE principles for Indigenous Data Governance complement FAIR by addressing power imbalances that FAIR does not. Collective Benefit requires that data ecosystems be designed to enable Indigenous Peoples to derive benefit from data. Authority to Control requires that the rights of Indigenous Peoples to govern the collection, use, and reuse of their data be recognised and supported. Responsibility requires that those working with Indigenous data nurture respectful relationships with Indigenous Peoples. Ethics requires that Indigenous Peoples’ rights and wellbeing be the primary concern at all stages of the data lifecycle.

The AIO’s governance framework is “aligned with the CARE and FAIR principles, ensuring that research involving Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples is guided by community authority and benefit-sharing”. Researchers using AIO tools to study content created by or about Indigenous communities must consider CARE principles alongside FAIR.

## The HREC Process

All research involving human participants or their data must be reviewed by a Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) or equivalent body at the researcher’s institution.

**Key Components of an Ethics Application**

An ethics application for digital media research should typically address the project description (what is being studied, why, and how, and what data will be collected), participant recruitment (how participants will be identified and approached, with inclusion and exclusion criteria), the consent process (how informed consent will be obtained, what information will be provided, and how consent will be documented), data collection (what tools will be used, what data categories will be collected, and how data minimisation will be practised at the file, field, and record levels), risk assessment (what the potential harms are, including to third parties, their likelihood, and the steps to mitigate them), data security (where data will be stored, who will have access, and what encryption and access controls will be used), de-identification (how personal identifiers will be removed, the known limitations of the approach, and residual re-identification risks), third-party data (how data about non-participants will be handled and whether collective consent mechanisms will be offered), data management (how long data will be retained, how it will be disposed of, and whether it will be shared with other researchers), and withdrawal (how participants can withdraw and what happens to their data if they do).

## Sample Ethics Application Language

The following sample paragraphs illustrate how key sections of an ethics application for digital media research might be drafted. These are templates that researchers should adapt to their specific study designs.

*Data Collection (DDP Studies)*

“Participants will be asked to request a copy of their personal data from [Platform Name] using the platform’s built-in data download feature. This data, known as a Data Download Package (DDP), will be uploaded to the AIO’s Data Donation Registration System (DDRS), a secure web-based platform hosted on Australian infrastructure. Before any data is transmitted to the research team, the DDRS presents participants with a complete listing of all data categories in their DDP, with checkboxes for each category. Participants may deselect any categories they do not wish to share. Only data that participants have explicitly approved is transferred. The research team will collect only the following data categories: [list specific categories]. All other data in the DDP will be discarded during the upload process and will not be stored or accessible to the research team.”

*Data Security*

“All donated data will be stored in the AIO Data Laboratory, which provides encrypted storage at rest and in transit, CILogon authentication for access control, role-based access restricting data to authorised project team members, and audit logging of all access and modification events. The infrastructure is hosted on Australian servers operated by [ARDC/institutional partner]. No data will be transferred outside Australia.”

*Third-Party Data*

“Donated DDPs may contain information about individuals other than the participant, including usernames of contacts, message content, tagged individuals, and algorithmically suggested profiles. These individuals have not consented to participate in the study. To protect their privacy, the following measures will be implemented: [1] Data categories containing conversational content (direct messages, group chat messages) will not be collected unless essential to the research question, and if collected, will be de-identified using [method]. [2] Third-party usernames and identifiers will be removed or replaced with pseudonymous codes during preprocessing. [3] No third-party data will be published in any form that could enable identification.”

*De-identification*

“De-identification will be performed in two stages. First, the DDRS applies field-level filtering at the point of donation, collecting only the data categories listed above. Second, the research team will apply [automated/manual] de-identification to the collected data, removing direct identifiers (usernames, full names, email addresses, phone numbers, URLs) and assessing indirect identifiers (timestamps, location data, device identifiers) for re-identification risk. The research team acknowledges that automated de-identification is imperfect: published evaluations of automated DDP de-identification pipelines (Boeschoten et al., 2021) report name detection recall of 0.56–0.90. Residual re-identification risk will be managed through secure storage, access controls, and the commitment not to attempt re-identification.”

## Data Retention and Destruction

Ethics applications must specify how long research data will be retained and how it will be disposed of. Digital media data raises particular issues because of the sensitivity and volume of the data involved.

**Retention Periods**

The Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research (2018) specifies that research data should generally be retained for a minimum of five years from the date of publication of the findings derived from the data. However, data involving sensitive personal information may warrant shorter retention if the ethics approval specifies this. Some institutions and funders require longer retention (e.g., clinical data, 15 years). The retention period should be specified in the ethics application and communicated to participants in the Participant Information Sheet.

**Secure Destruction**

When the retention period expires, data must be securely destroyed. For digital data, this means permanent deletion from all storage locations (primary storage, backups, local copies, cloud storage), secure deletion using file shredding tools that overwrite data rather than simply removing file pointers, destruction of any physical media (USB drives, external hard drives) that contained the data, and documentation of the destruction process, including what was destroyed, when, by whom, and using what method. The AIO Data Laboratory can implement scheduled data destruction for projects hosted on its infrastructure. Researchers using their own storage must implement destruction procedures independently.

**What Happens to Participant Data on Withdrawal**

If a participant withdraws from a study, their data should be deleted unless the ethics protocol specifies otherwise (e.g., where data has already been de-identified and aggregated to the point where individual records cannot be isolated). The withdrawal process and its implications for data should be clearly communicated to participants in the consent materials.

**Data Security and Infrastructure**

Ethical data management requires technical infrastructure that supports the commitments made in ethics applications. The AIO Data Laboratory provides CILogon authentication for institutional single sign-on ensuring identity verification, role-based access control restricting data access to authorised project members with defined roles, encrypted storage with data encrypted at rest and in transit, Australian-hosted infrastructure addressing data sovereignty concerns, audit logging of access and modification events for accountability, and separation of environments so that raw data, processed data, and analytical outputs are maintained in separate environments with distinct access controls.

## Data Donation: Full Informed Consent

Data donation is the method most closely aligned with traditional informed consent. Participants are recruited before data collection begins, receive a Participant Information Sheet explaining the study, understand what data is being requested and how it will be used, can review their personal data before donating it, can selectively exclude data categories they are not comfortable sharing, and can withdraw at any time. The AIO’s Data Relay is designed to support each of these requirements. The data review step, where participants inspect their data with checkboxes for each category, is a particularly strong mechanism for operationalising informed consent.
