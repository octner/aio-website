How can observatories help researchers and wider civil society better understand, scrutinise, and hold digital platforms to account? This was the question at the heart of *Building Digital Platform Observatories*, a one-day event hosted by the Centre for Digital Cultures and Societies at The University of Queensland. The event invited attendees to think about the future of Humanities and Social Science & Indigenous Studies (HASS & I) research infrastructure in a world increasingly mediated by digital platforms. The event brought together researchers working across digital cultures, platform governance, data access and research infrastructure to consider how observatories might support independent scrutiny of powerful platform systems.

Digital platform observatories functionally bring together methods, tools and infrastructures researchers use to study how digital platforms shape public life, from content moderation and recommendation systems to advertising, data access and governance. More significantly, observatories can produce *observability* into the impacts of platforms on civil society. Bernhard Rieder and Jeanette Hofmann use the term [observability](https://policyreview.info/articles/analysis/towards-platform-observability), rather than transparency, to emphasise the need to “address the conditions, means, and processes of knowledge production of large-scale socio-technical systems” in holding digital platforms to account. As public institutions, observatories have the potential to enable everyday practices of observability among the public; frameworks of governance and regulation; and the production of trustworthy observations by researchers.

Presentations highlighted that an observatory is not only a set of research methods or tools, but also a “broader sensibility” as described by opening speaker Professor Liz McFall from the University of Edinburgh. Speakers throughout the day expanded on this sensibility, framing it as grounded in the shared vision of platform accountability and its importance to contemporary democratic and social life.

Realising this vision is no small feat. Professor James Smithies from the Australian National University highlighted the immense challenge that HASS&I disciplines face in developing and maintaining technical infrastructure needed to observe digital platforms. James emphasised that funding, resources, and fostering expertise are essential to developing HASS digital research infrastructure that can meaningfully hold global platform companies, with their near-limitless development resources, to account.

Across the day, presentations showcased current work researchers are undertaking in platform observability. Although this work can at times be improvised, messy, cumbersome, and time-consuming, it is precisely this collective effort that lays the groundwork for more robust and effective forms of platform observability.

Dr Nichola Burton, Programs Architect for the ARDC HASS&I Data Commons, expressed the value of the observatory for digital platform research: “It was great to have that in-depth discussion of the “why” of observatories like the AIO – both in terms of the importance of this capability for research and policy, and also in terms of the struggles that researchers currently face when trying to access the data. These kinds of discussions are really important because they help us to maintain a clear vision of the purpose of this infrastructure, who it serves and its benefits.”

“The event showed us the thriving connections developing between the AIO and researchers working on cutting-edge projects about our digital cultures and societies,” reflected Professor Nicholas Carah, AIO Deputy Director. “There’s deep thinking happening that is transforming how we understand observability ‘in the wild’ as improvised and relational practices. All of this is informing our sense of what it means to build an observatory as an important national institution.”

The discussions will help inform ongoing efforts to build the AIO infrastructure that supports independent research, public accountability and democratic oversight of digital platforms.

## Event details

The [Centre for Digital Cultures and Societies (DCS)](https://hass.uq.edu.au/centre-digital-cultures-societies) hosted the one-day symposium **Building Digital Platform Observatories** on 16 June 2026, 9:00am–5:00pm, at the UQ Brisbane City Campus, 308 Queen Street, Brisbane, in partnership with the Australian Internet Observatory.

The program featured presentations from researchers including [Liz McFall (University of Edinburgh)](https://www.sps.ed.ac.uk/staff/liz-mcfall), [James Smithies (Australian National University)](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/persons/james-smithies/), [Daniel Angus (Queensland University of Technology)](https://www.qut.edu.au/about/our-people/academic-profiles/daniel.angus), [Kellie Vella (University of Queensland)](https://www.qut.edu.au/about/our-people/academic-profiles/kellie.vella), and [Laura Vodden (Queensland University of Technology)](https://research.qut.edu.au/dmrc/people/laura-vodden/), alongside sessions led by AIO collaborators [Nic Carah](https://communication-arts.uq.edu.au/profile/363/nicholas-carah) and [Julian Thomas](https://www.rmit.edu.au/profiles/t/julian-thomas).

The event was hosted by DCS in partnership with AIO. The AIO is a co-investment partnership with the Australian Research Data Commons (ARDC) through the HASS and Indigenous Research Data Commons ([DOI: 10.3565/hjrp-b141](https://doi.org/10.3565/hjrp-b141)). The ARDC is enabled by the Australian Government’s National Collaborative Research Infrastructure Strategy (NCRIS).
